# All 64 Hexagram Meanings — Quick Reference Guide to the I Ching

The **I Ching** (易经, Yì Jīng), or *Book of Changes*, contains 64 hexagrams — six-line symbols that represent archetypal situations and phases of change. Each hexagram carries layered meanings that shift depending on which lines are changing, but at its core, each one describes a fundamental pattern of human experience.

This guide provides a **complete reference** of all 64 hexagram meanings, presented in the traditional King Wen sequence. Whether you're looking up a specific hexagram after a reading or studying the full system, this quick-reference table gives you the essential meaning of every hexagram in one place.

> **Pro tip:** If you've just cast a hexagram and want to know what it means, read this guide first, then go deeper by studying the specific hexagram's full interpretation — including its changing lines and transformed hexagram.

## Understanding This Reference

Each entry below includes:
- **Hexagram number and name** — in English, Chinese, and pinyin
- **Trigram composition** — upper over lower trigram
- **Core meaning** — a concise one-to-two sentence interpretation

The hexagrams are presented in the **King Wen sequence** (序卦, Xì Guà), the traditional ordering that reflects a philosophical narrative of how situations transform into one another. This is the sequence used in virtually all I Ching translations and divination tools.

## How to Use This Reference

### After a Reading

If you've cast a hexagram and want to understand what it means:

1. **Find your hexagram** in the table above by number
2. **Read the core meaning** to get the essential theme
3. **Study the full text** — look up the complete interpretation including the Judgment and Image for depth
4. **Check changing lines** — if any lines changed, each has its own specific meaning that modifies the general reading
5. **Consider the transformed hexagram** — if lines changed, look up the hexagram it transforms into

### For Study and Meditation

The King Wen sequence is not random. Each hexagram flows into the next through a relationship of contrast, complement, or transformation. For example:

- **Hexagram 1 (Creative) and Hexagram 2 (Receptive)** are opposites — pure yang and pure yin
- **Hexagram 11 (Peace) and Hexagram 12 (Standstill)** are inverses — one shows harmony, the other shows blockage
- **Hexagram 63 (After Completion) and Hexagram 64 (Before Completion)** form the final pair, suggesting that completion immediately gives way to a new cycle of becoming

Many practitioners find it useful to study the hexagrams in pairs, exploring how each situation contains the seed of its opposite.

### Understanding the Changing Lines

Every hexagram has six lines, numbered from bottom to top. Each line can be:
- **Stable** (young yin or young yang) — the energy is consistent
- **Changing** (old yin or old yang) — the energy is shifting and will flip to its opposite

When a line changes, it adds a layer of specificity to the reading. The same hexagram can have very different interpretations depending on which lines are changing. This is why a quick-reference table, while useful for general orientation, cannot replace the full hexagram text with its line-specific guidance.

## The Philosophy Behind the Hexagrams

What makes the 64 hexagrams more than a simple fortune-telling list is their underlying philosophy:

**Every situation is temporary.** No hexagram represents a permanent state. Even Hexagram 11 (Peace) contains the seed of Hexagram 12 (Standstill), and even Hexagram 64 (Before Completion) points toward a new beginning.

**Context matters.** A hexagram's meaning shifts depending on the question asked, the changing lines present, and the broader circumstances of the seeker. The same hexagram might offer very different guidance to a business leader and to an artist.

**Action is always possible.** The I Ching never presents a situation as hopeless. Even in the darkest hexagrams (like Hexagram 36, Darkening of the Light), there is guidance on how to navigate the difficulty with integrity.

### What are changing lines and why do they matter?

Changing lines are the "active" lines in your hexagram — they indicate aspects of your situation that are currently shifting. When a line changes, it flips from yin to yang or vice versa, creating a second (transformed) hexagram. The changing line texts provide the most specific guidance for your question, while the transformed hexagram shows the likely direction of events.

### Is there a "best" or "worst" hexagram?

No. The I Ching does not have good or bad hexagrams in an absolute sense — only appropriate or inappropriate for a given situation. A hexagram that seems challenging may contain the exact guidance you need to navigate a difficult period wisely.

### Can I use this table without casting a hexagram?

Yes — many people study the 64 hexagrams as a framework for understanding life patterns. Each hexagram represents an archetype of human experience, and reflecting on them can provide deep insight even without a specific divination question.
